How this works, in plain language

You do your job for a week. Something quietly notes which programs you used and how long each thing took — never what you typed. At the end you get a document that says what your work costs and what to automate first.

Why not just ask people?

Because the answer is always a guess, and everything after it inherits the guess.

Asking

“Invoicing? Maybe four hours a week.”

  • Rounded to a story, not a stopwatch
  • Leaves out the spreadsheet nobody mentions
  • Never mentions copying numbers between two screens, because that is remembered as part of the job, not as a job
Watching

“Ten point six hours a week each, across two people, over eighteen observed runs.”

  • A measurement you can put in a business case
  • Finds every program the work actually touches
  • Catches the copy-paste between systems — usually the most expensive habit in the building

The two rules that keep it honest

Rule one

Time is measured. Money is typed by a person.

A browser can time your work. It cannot know what anyone is paid. So if nobody enters an hourly rate, that job shows its hours and contributes zero dollars — and the report says so by name, instead of quietly using a industry average and passing it off as your number.

Rule two

A short week is reported, never stretched.

If we only watched for two days, we do not multiply by three and a half to make a week. That would turn a guess into something that looks exactly like a measurement. Two days is reported as two days, marked low confidence.

And it refuses to finish if you skip something. Miss one of the six questions and it will not produce a roadmap around the gap. It stops and tells you which answer is missing. A confident document built on things nobody asked is exactly what most executives have already been sold once.
